# Review the Song, Not Just the Counts

Use this alongside the writer and reviewer checklists. The MCP text tools supply
evidence for those reviews; they do not perform the musical judgment themselves.
Use [review-scenarios.md](review-scenarios.md) to exercise the cross-skill routes
after changing this method. These scenarios do not replace a musical audition.

## Start With the Dramatic Reason

Before changing wording, identify who is speaking, to whom, what they want, and
what complicates that want. A documentary narrator need not become an invented
first-person witness. Preserve the distinction between sourced history and
disclosed fictional staging.

Ask what changes in each verse and what the returning chorus now means. Trace
an important image or action through the song. Keep meaningful recurrence;
revise redundant explanation. A chorus can repeat exactly while the intervening
story changes its force. Plain needs and elevated imagery can coexist.

Review a complete draft in context before optimizing isolated lines. The review
must identify weaknesses as well as strengths; a persuasive explanation of a
draft is not evidence that someone would want to sing it.

## Separate Four Kinds of Evidence

| Evidence | Can establish | Cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spelling-based counts | Estimated line length and count dispersion | Pronunciation, stress, meter, groove or singability |
| Spoken scansion | A plausible pronunciation and verbal stress reading | Actual note positions, melody or a successful performance |
| Authored rhythmic/melodic setting | Specified entrances, durations, rests, contour and section contrast | A singer's comfort or the listener's emotional response |
| Identified performed take | How that version actually handles the words and arrangement | That every other take or future generation will do the same |

`count_syllables` labels whole-input count dispersion CONSISTENT or UNEVEN.
Neither is a quality verdict. Check count disagreements by pronunciation, not
by rewriting words to satisfy the heuristic. A melisma adds notes, not lyric
syllables. A sung contraction may differ from an ordinary spoken estimate.

## Give Each Section a Musical Setting

1. Read the whole song and any existing score, recording or timing ledger.
   Record which version supplies the reference; do not call an imagined setting
   a transcription. Compare phrase allocations as well as words. Halving a
   phrase's bars at the same tempo doubles its density even when total song
   duration stays unchanged.
2. Identify the section's job and its relation to the neighboring sections.
   Fewer words alone do not create a lift; a high note alone does not create a
   climax. Decide what should become more exposed, insistent, joyful or unresolved.
3. Check natural stress and pronunciation for every line. Corresponding lines
   need not have identical syllable counts, but any reused melody must accommodate
   their differences without accidental stress changes or cramming.
4. For the phrases under review, state pulse/tempo, meter, subdivision, pickups,
   phrase spans, accents, held vowels, releases and rests. Give concrete syllable
   placements for risky or changed phrases, including the entrance and exit.
   Do not borrow time silently from the adjacent section. A poetic foot is not
   a musical bar. Stress can be expressed by duration, pitch, articulation or
   syncopation; it need not always fall on beats 1 and 3.
5. Compose or inspect a melodic motif and section contrast when those are the
   problem. A brief score, MIDI or guide recording can test pitch and rhythm;
   it need not prescribe the entire production. A keyboard guide is not a vocal
   performance. A style prompt is a request, not evidence the model followed it.
6. Check the whole transition, not an isolated line. Test changed final choruses
   separately. A technically valid grid can still sound square or unmemorable.

## Diagnose Before Rewriting

If the words carry a strong relationship or image, first compare musical settings
with those words held fixed. If the wording is awkward independent of melody,
revise it and log the reason. Preserve originals and user-protected passages;
offer separate alternatives where a substantive change needs a decision.

Do not treat a familiar rhyme, parallel phrase, repeated title or irregular count
as an automatic defect. Ask what it does in this song. Likewise, do not invent a
post-hoc scansion merely to defend weak writing. The analysis should lead to a
testable choice, not praise every line indiscriminately.

## Report What Was Actually Done

Use a concise section ledger in the review, not a new track-status system:

Identify the final reviewed input versions/hashes after any auto-applied
pronunciation edits: lyrics/structure, style, exclusions, generation settings,
phrase allocation/score and reference audio (including explicit absence).
When an input changes, re-review the affected passages and transitions rather
than carrying a stale report forward. Keep text review, proposed composition,
identified performance and human permissions separate.

| Section/version | Dramatic function | Stress/phrase issue | Setting or change tested | Evidence and open decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-chorus, draft A | Anticipation before the request | Ending crowds the next pickup | Same words, longer release | Proposed timing only; not performed |

Cover every sung section, explicitly referencing repeated settings and separately
checking changed returns. Distinguish text review, proposed setting and performed
listening. Name the audio/version if one was actually heard. Without a take,
say **performance untested**, never "sung and verified." An instrument-only
guide can be assessed for composition, not for breath or vocal pronunciation.

Before scaling a batch, compare a whole pilot transition or song with the user's
ear. Do not apply one newly invented rhythmic template to every genre. Source
approval and spending/listening consent remain independent of creative review.

## Background

- [Pat Pattison on prosody](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/prosody-in-music-and-songwriting/): lyric and musical choices serve the same emotional purpose.
- [Andrea Stolpe on melodic tools](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/simple-tools-for-better-melodies/): motif, repetition, note lengths, rests and contrast matter alongside pitch.
